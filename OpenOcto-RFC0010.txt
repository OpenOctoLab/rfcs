                       OCP over Secure Transport

Abstract

   This document specifies how a session of the Octopus Control Protocol
   [RFC0004] is carried inside a secure transport instead of being
   protected by the frame-layer primitives of [RFC0009]: OCP over TLS,
   OCP over DTLS and OCP over QUIC.  It gives the ALPN identifier, the
   port and the URI scheme, the framing rules of each transport, the
   mapping of tunnel traffic and of loss-tolerant traffic onto QUIC
   streams and QUIC datagrams, the authentication of a session by
   binding it to the transport, and the mapping between transport
   failures and protocol failures.  It specifies no cryptography and no
   frame-layer primitive: everything it protects is protected by the
   transport, and a session that uses it does not use [RFC0009] at the
   same time.

1.  Introduction

   [RFC0004] carries cleartext and names no cipher.  It leaves the
   protection of a session to one of two extensions, and this document
   is one of them: instead of adding primitives to the frames, it puts
   the whole session inside a transport that already provides
   confidentiality, integrity and an authenticated peer.  Nothing in
   [RFC0004] changes.  The frame layer is the core frame layer, with the
   header of sixteen octets and no trailer, and every message, primitive
   and rule of that document applies as it does over TCP.

   Three bindings are specified: OCP over TLS (Section 5), for a stream
   transport; OCP over DTLS (Section 6), for a datagram transport that a
   device already has; and OCP over QUIC (Section 7), for a transport
   that offers streams and datagrams over one connection.  What applies
   to all three is in Section 4.  TLS and DTLS are the two halves of the
   same handshake over different carriers, so most of Section 5  applies
   to both and Section 6 states only what is different.

   The benefit of doing it here rather than in the frame layer is that
   the work is already done, reviewed and implemented: a deployment gets
   peer authentication, forward secrecy, key update, replay protection
   and congestion control without specifying any of them, and a device
   that has a TLS library needs no new code.  The cost is that what is
   protected is the transport and not the frame: a peer that holds the
   transport keys sees everything, a relay that terminates the transport
   sees everything, and no frame carries a proof of its own that
   outlives the session.

1.1.  Two Ways to Protect a Session

   [RFC0009] adds integrity, encryption and zero-trust verification to
   the frames themselves.  This document wraps the session in a secure
   transport.  The two solve the same problem at different layers, and a
   session uses one of them.

   *  An endpoint that runs OCP over a secure transport MUST NOT offer
      the header form, or any algorithm, of [RFC0009]: it MUST NOT send
      the "hdr" member and MUST NOT send the "enc", "itg" or "ztr" keys
      of "algs".

   *  An endpoint whose WELCOME confirms any of them anyway MUST close
      the session.  It SHOULD first send ERROR with E_UNSUPPORTED.

   The reason is not that the combination is fatal but that it is
   wasteful and misleading: a payload would be encrypted twice, the
   inner tag would authenticate octets the outer one already
   authenticates, and an operator reading the negotiation would not be
   able to tell which layer is actually protecting the session.  Which
   of the two a deployment wants is a matter of what it can change: this
   document when it can change how the connection is made, [RFC0009]
   when it cannot, or when frames must be verifiable on their own or
   survive being stored and replayed to a third party.

2.  Conventions

   The key words of [IETF-RFC2119] and [IETF-RFC8174] apply.  [RFC0004]
   is normative for this document; a term defined there has the same
   meaning here.  "Secure transport" means TLS as specified in Section
   5, DTLS as specified in Section 6, or QUIC as specified in Section
   7.  The first two are together called "the TLS family" where the
   distinction does not matter.  "Initiator" and "responder" are the
   roles of [RFC0004], Section 6, and "client" and "server" are the
   roles of the transport: the initiator is the client.

3.  The Model

   One transport connection carries exactly one OCP session, and one OCP
   session occupies exactly one transport connection.

   *  The client is the initiator: it sends HELLO.  The server is the
      responder: it sends WELCOME.

   *  The transport is established first.  HELLO is the first
      application octet the client writes and WELCOME is the first the
      server writes; no OCP frame precedes the handshake and no
      transport message is an OCP message.

   *  A lost transport connection ends the session.  There is no
      resumption of a session across connections: the next connection
      opens a new session with a new HELLO, as [RFC0004], Section 6.3,
      requires of a reconnect.  Byte streams resume by the tunnel offset
      of [RFC0004], Section 6.6.4, which is an application matter and
      survives either way.

   *  The frame layer is the core frame layer of [RFC0004], Section 3.1:
      sixteen octets, "header_len" equal to 0x0010, no trailer.  A
      receiver MUST reject with E_UNSUPPORTED a frame whose "header_len"
      is anything else.

   What the transport provides, and what it does not:

   *  It provides confidentiality and integrity of everything inside it,
      including the frame header, so "type", "seq" and "payload_len" are
      not visible outside the transport.

   *  It provides an authenticated peer.  The server is always
      authenticated; the client is authenticated only when it presents
      a certificate or when the binding of Section 4.5 is used.

   *  It does not authorise anything.  Knowing who a peer is, and
      deciding what that peer may do, are different questions, and the
      second belongs to the application.

4.  Common Requirements

4.1.  Transport Versions

   *  TLS: TLS 1.3 [IETF-RFC8446] SHOULD be used.  TLS 1.2
      [IETF-RFC5246] MAY be used when the peer does not implement 1.3,
      under the conditions of Section 5.3.  Every version before 1.2
      MUST NOT be used.

   *  DTLS: DTLS 1.3 [IETF-RFC9147] SHOULD be used.  DTLS 1.2
      [IETF-RFC6347] MAY be used under the same conditions, which apply
      to DTLS as they do to TLS.  Every version before 1.2 MUST NOT be
      used.  DTLS is OPTIONAL: a deployment that needs a datagram
      transport and has QUIC uses QUIC (Section 7) instead, and one that
      has a DTLS stack and no QUIC uses DTLS.

   *  QUIC: QUIC version 1 [IETF-RFC9000] with the TLS 1.3 mapping of
      [IETF-RFC9001].  The unreliable datagram extension [IETF-RFC9221]
      is OPTIONAL and is required only by Section 7.4.

   An endpoint that cannot meet the lowest version permitted here MUST
   refuse the connection rather than negotiate less.

   A version is not chosen by OCP and is not marked in any frame: it is
   chosen by the transport, and the OCP session is the same either way.
   What differs is what the transport offers for the same money, which
   Section 5.3 sets out.

4.2.  The ALPN Identifier

   The initiator offers the ALPN identifier [IETF-RFC7301] "ocp/1" and
   the responder selects it.  A responder that does not select it does
   not speak OCP over this transport, and the initiator MUST close the
   connection instead of sending HELLO.

   The identifier names the protocol and its major version.  A change to
   [RFC0004] that is not compatible increments the version octet of
   [RFC0004] and the second element here together.

4.3.  Ports and URI Scheme

   +=============+============+========================================+
   |Transport    |Port        |Meaning                                 |
   +=============+============+========================================+
   |TCP          |6286        |OCP in cleartext [RFC0004], Section 7.4.|
   +-------------+------------+----------------------------------------+
   |Frame-sec    |6357        |Frame-layer secure OCP (scheme ocp-fls) |
   +-------------+------------+----------------------------------------+
   |TLS over TCP |16286       |OCP over TLS (Section 5).               |
   +-------------+------------+----------------------------------------+
   |QUIC         |16286 (UDP) |OCP over QUIC (Section 7).              |
   +-------------+------------+----------------------------------------+
   |DTLS over UDP|16286 (UDP) |OCP over DTLS (Section 6).              |
   +-------------+------------+----------------------------------------+

                          Table 1: Ports

   The RECOMMENDED port for a secure transport is 16286, for all three
   bindings.  The digits are a mnemonic in the same way as those of
   [RFC0004]: on a telephone keypad the first key carries no letters,
   and the remaining four spell O, C, T and O.  The leading 1 is what
   lets the secure port be the same word as the cleartext one, 6286,
   without being the same number.  The value is a convenience and not an
   assignment; a deployment MAY use any port, and an endpoint learns the
   port of its peer from configuration or from service discovery, never
   from the protocol.

   One number serves three bindings, and none of the three is in
   conflict with another.

   *  TLS over TCP and the two datagram bindings are on different
      carriers, so the number means two different sockets and there is
      nothing to share.

   *  QUIC and DTLS are both on UDP and do share the socket.  They are
      told apart by the first octet of the packet, which is what the
      transports themselves already place there:

      -  A QUIC packet of version 1 always has the
         second-most-significant bit of its first octet set, so the
         octet is in 0x40 to 0x7F for a short header and in 0xC0 to 0xFF
         for a long one.

      -  A DTLS record begins with a content type, whose values are
         below 0x40, so the octet is in 0x14 to 0x19.

      The ranges do not overlap, and the test is one comparison.  An
      endpoint that serves both on one socket MUST dispatch on that
      octet and MUST NOT guess from what it served last; an endpoint
      that serves one MAY still check, and SHOULD ignore a packet that
      is neither.

   One URI scheme is defined here.  It is used by configuration files,
   by tools and by logs [RFC0007], is not part of the protocol, and no
   endpoint parses one at run time.

      ocps://host[:port]     secure; port defaults to 16286

      The host is an IPv4 or IPv6 address, or a host name.

   The scheme of a cleartext session, "ocp", belongs to [RFC0004],
   Section 7.4, and is not repeated here.  A tool that is given "ocps"
   MUST use a secure transport and MUST NOT fall back to cleartext when
   the handshake fails, whether by opening a cleartext connection or by
   rewriting the scheme to "ocp".

4.4.  Peer Identity

   The transport authenticates the server always, and the client when
   one of Section 4.5 is used.  How an identity is checked is
   [IETF-RFC6125] with these additions.

   *  The server certificate MUST be validated against a trust anchor
      that the deployment chose, and against the host name the client
      meant to reach.  A client MUST NOT skip validation, and MUST NOT
      accept a certificate it cannot name a reason to trust.

   *  The client SHOULD send the Server Name Indication of the host it
      means to reach.  A server that is reached with no SNI, or with a
      name it does not serve, MAY refuse the connection.

   *  A device deployment SHOULD pin: an endpoint that knows the
      certificate, or the public key, of the peer it expects accepts
      nothing else.  Pinning is what makes a robot reachable without a
      public certificate authority and without a name in DNS.

   *  A server that needs to know who called it requires a client
      certificate or the binding of Section 4.5.  Without one of the two
      it knows only that somebody connected, and an application MUST NOT
      take the "client" member of HELLO as an identity: that member is
      free-form text for logging.

   *  F_AUTH is set on the session when the server's identity has been
      verified, which in this document is always.  An endpoint that also
      needs the initiator's identity verified uses one of Section 4.5
      and MUST NOT treat F_AUTH as saying more than it does.

4.5.  Authentication and Channel Binding

   The secure transport already proves who the peers are, so the "auth"
   member of [RFC0004], Section 6.2, is OPTIONAL.  It has one job here
   that the transport does not do by itself: proving that the OCP
   session and the transport belong to the same peer, which an endpoint
   needs when the transport is terminated in front of it, as by a relay
   (Section 5.6), or when it wants a proof it can log.

   The RECOMMENDED method for that is "tls-exporter".

      {"method": "tls-exporter", "nonce": h'...'}
      mac = HMAC-SHA256(exporter, "OCP-HELLO" || nonce)

      {"ok": true, "nonce": h'...', "mac": h'...'}
      mac = HMAC-SHA256(exporter, "OCP-WELCOME" || c_nonce || s_nonce)

   The exporter is the keying material exporter of the TLS session
   [IETF-RFC5705], as defined for TLS 1.3 by [IETF-RFC8446], taken with
   the label "EXPORTER-OCP-channel-binding", an empty context and a
   length of 32 octets.  The same label and context are used over DTLS
   and over QUIC, where the exporter of the TLS session that QUIC
   carries [IETF-RFC9001] is taken.  The nonce of the initiator is at
   least sixteen octets of unpredictable data; the nonce of the
   responder is generated the same way.

   The value of the exercise is that the exporter is known only to the
   two ends of the TLS session.  A peer that is not the endpoint of that
   session cannot compute the MAC, so a relay that terminates TLS and
   opens a new one cannot forward a credential it did not have, and a
   credential cannot be moved from one transport session to another.

   A method that reveals a long-lived secret in the credential, as a
   shared token does, is unnecessary here and SHOULD NOT be used: the
   transport already hides the octets, and a secret that crosses the
   wire only invites it to be logged.

4.6.  Framing

   TLS and QUIC deliver the payload of a frame in order and without
   gaps, and present it as octets: TLS as a stream and QUIC as one or
   more streams.  DTLS delivers datagrams, and Section 6.2 states the
   rules that apply there instead.  For the two that deliver a stream,
   the framing rules are those of [RFC0004], Section 7.1.

   *  Frames are written back to back with no padding and no separator.
      The header states "header_len" and "payload_len", which is all a
      receiver needs.

   *  The units of the transport are not the units of the protocol.  A
      frame MAY be split across several TLS records or QUIC STREAM
      frames, and several frames MAY share one.  A receiver MUST NOT
      assume that one read is one frame and MUST parse by length.

   *  A sender SHOULD write one frame per transport write when the
      frame is small and its delivery is latency sensitive, so that a
      caller is not delayed behind a bulk transfer; it SHOULD coalesce
      when it is sending tunnel data.

   *  Nothing here changes the limit of [RFC0004], Section 3.3.  A frame
      larger than the negotiated "max_payload" is still
      E_PAYLOAD_TOO_LARGE.

4.7.  Compression

   Compression is negotiated and applied exactly as in [RFC0004],
   Section 3.5, and is unaffected by the transport.  It compresses the
   payload before the transport protects it, which is the only place it
   can: the transport would defeat it otherwise.

   TLS compression, which is where the attacks on compressed secrets
   came from, does not exist in TLS 1.3 and MUST NOT be negotiated in
   any version.

5.  OCP over TLS

5.1.  Establishing a Session

   1.  The client connects and completes the TLS handshake, at the
       highest version both ends implement, offering "ocp/1" through
       ALPN.

   2.  The client validates the server certificate as in Section 4.4,
       and presents a client certificate, or sends the credential of
       Section 4.5, when the deployment requires one.

   3.  The client writes HELLO as its first application octets.  The
       server, whose first frame was not HELLO, answers E_HELLO_EXPECTED
       as [RFC0004] requires.

   4.  The server writes WELCOME, and the session proceeds as in
       [RFC0004], Section 6.

   A session that cannot meet the requirements of Section 4.1 is not
   established: the handshake fails before any OCP frame is sent, and
   the failure is a transport failure and not an OCP error.

5.2.  Frames and Records

   TLS delivers a stream of octets, and the rules of Section 4.6 apply.
   Two points are particular to it.

   *  The size of a TLS record is not the size of a frame.  A sender
      SHOULD let a large frame, mostly tunnel data, span several records
      rather than buffer it, and SHOULD let a group of small frames
      share one record rather than write each separately.  A deployment
      that wants to hide the boundaries between frames SHOULD do the
      latter.

   *  A sender MUST NOT rely on a receiver seeing the same record
      boundaries, and a receiver MUST NOT infer a frame boundary from
      one.

5.3.  Version Fallback

   TLS 1.3 is what a deployment should use, and TLS 1.2 is permitted
   because a device that cannot be updated should not be unreachable: a
   robot that speaks only 1.2 is still a robot that has to be
   controlled, and the alternative to accepting it is usually to turn
   the protection off.  Falling back is therefore allowed, and is
   allowed only under all of the following.

   1.  The client MUST offer TLS 1.3 first and MUST fall back only when
       the handshake with 1.3 fails, never by preferring 1.2.  A
       deployment that configures 1.2 directly is not falling back and
       is not what this section permits.

   2.  The cipher suite MUST be an AEAD suite.  CBC, stream ciphers, and
       every suite without an authenticated cipher MUST NOT be used.

   3.  Renegotiation MUST NOT be used, and the extended master secret of
       [IETF-RFC7627] MUST be negotiated.  Section 5.4 states why.

   4.  TLS compression MUST NOT be used, which Section 4.7 already
       requires of every version.

   5.  The version actually negotiated SHOULD be recorded with the
       session, so that an operator can see which sessions are on 1.2
       and why.  An endpoint MAY refuse to serve a device it knows can
       do better.

   What 1.2 costs: no 0-RTT, no key update after the handshake without
   renegotiation, and a handshake that takes one more round trip.  None
   of it changes OCP.  A session over either version carries the same
   frames, the same messages and the same session identifier, and an
   implementation MUST NOT make a protocol decision on the version of
   the transport.

5.4.  Rekeying and Renegotiation

   *  A TLS key update does not affect the OCP session in any way: no
      frame marks it, no key of [RFC0004] or of [RFC0009] is involved,
      and a session needs no action.

   *  TLS renegotiation MUST NOT be used.  It does not exist in TLS 1.3,
      and an implementation that offers it over a version that has it
      MUST refuse it.  What a renegotiation was used for, rotation of
      the key or a second authentication, is done here by a key update
      or by opening a new session.

   *  A long-lived session that wants a new key rotates the transport
      key: the client or the server sends a key update, which TLS 1.2
      does not have, so over 1.2 the endpoint closes the connection and
      opens a new session.

5.5.  Closure

   *  A graceful close sends BYE and then close_notify.  The peer
      answers nothing: [RFC0004] asks for no acknowledgement of BYE, and
      the transport close confirms it.

   *  A close_notify with no BYE ends the session just the same.  An
      endpoint SHOULD send BYE first when it can, so that the peer can
      log a reason.

   *  An abrupt close, by a reset or by a timeout, ends the session.
      Outstanding calls are unanswered; a caller that needs an answer
      asks again on the next session.

   *  A session MUST NOT be used after the transport is closed.  An
      implementation that keeps OCP state longer than the transport MUST
      discard it.

5.6.  Relays

   A relay that terminates TLS and opens a new TLS connection carries
   two OCP sessions, one on each side, and is a peer of both.

   *  It sees every frame in cleartext and is trusted accordingly.  A
      deployment that places one between a program and a device has
      decided that the relay is part of the device.

   *  It MUST NOT change a frame it forwards.  It MAY change the
      negotiated properties of the session, and when it does the two
      sessions are not the same session and it MUST establish the far
      one itself with its own HELLO.

   *  The binding of Section 4.5 does not survive a relay that
      terminates TLS, which is the point of it: the exporter of the far
      session is not the exporter of the near one.

6.  OCP over DTLS

   DTLS is the datagram half of TLS [IETF-RFC9147], [IETF-RFC6347].  It
   is OPTIONAL, and it is here for a device that has a DTLS stack and no
   QUIC: it gives the same handshake, the same certificates and the same
   exporter, and it costs less code than adding QUIC.  A deployment that
   has QUIC and no constraint prefers QUIC (Section 7), which adds
   streams, tunnels on their own streams, and better loss recovery.

   Everything in Section 5 applies, with the differences below and with
   the rules of [RFC0004], Section 7.2, for the frames it carries.

6.1.  Establishing a Session

   The client completes the DTLS handshake, offering "ocp/1" through
   ALPN, and validates the server certificate as in Section 4.4.  The
   version is chosen as in Section 4.1, and the fallback rules of
   Section 5.3 apply to DTLS 1.2 as they do to TLS 1.2.  The client then
   writes HELLO as its first application datagram and the server answers
   WELCOME.  The socket is the port of Section 4.3, which DTLS shares
   with QUIC: the first octet of a packet says which transport it
   belongs to, and a server that serves both dispatches on it.

   *  The cookie exchange of DTLS MUST be used.  It is what keeps a
      server from being made to answer for an address it has never seen,
      which for a device on a local network is the only amplification
      defence there is (Section 9).  A server that receives a
      ClientHello with no cookie answers HelloVerifyRequest.

   *  The handshake is retransmitted by DTLS itself.  The application is
      not involved and MUST NOT retry HELLO for that reason; it retries
      only under the rules of [RFC0004], Section 7.2.

   *  HELLO and WELCOME are application data and follow the handshake.
      A session is established only when the handshake is complete, and
      a frame that arrives before it MUST be discarded.

6.2.  Datagrams and Frames

   One datagram carries exactly one frame, and the frame is complete:
   "payload_len" is in the header, so nothing is reassembled and DTLS
   needs no framing of its own.

   *  A frame, including the header and the DTLS overhead, SHOULD NOT
      exceed 1200 octets, so that it does not have to be fragmented and
      so that losing one fragment does not lose the frame.

   *  A sender MUST NOT send a frame larger than the path MTU it knows,
      and SHOULD discover that MTU rather than assume one.

   *  Bulk data does not belong on DTLS.  A tunnel MAY run over it, one
      window at a time, but a deployment that moves firmware uses TLS or
      QUIC instead, where a stream carries it.

   *  F_ACK, the retransmission of a frame with the same sequence
      number, and the deduplication of a frame that arrives twice are as
      [RFC0004], Section 7.2, requires.

6.3.  Replay, Loss and Retransmission

   *  DTLS applies its own replay protection to the records it carries,
      within a sliding window.  That protects the transport and not the
      application: a receiver SHOULD still reject a frame whose "seq" it
      has already accepted, unless the octets are identical to the frame
      it accepted under that number.

   *  DTLS does not retransmit application data.  A frame that must
      arrive is acknowledged and repeated by OCP: the sender sets F_ACK
      and repeats the frame with the same sequence number on timeout.

   *  A datagram that is lost is lost.  A session that loses more than
      it can tolerate SHOULD be moved to a stream transport rather than
      be asked to retry harder.

6.4.  Closure

   *  A graceful close sends BYE and then close_notify.

   *  A close_notify with no BYE ends the session just the same.

   *  DTLS has no reset, so a session can also end by silence: an
      endpoint MAY abandon a datagram session that has been quiet, as
      [RFC0004], Section 7.2, permits.  The RECOMMENDED silence before
      abandoning one is three times the heartbeat interval of [RFC0004],
      Section 6.7.

   *  A session MUST NOT be used after the transport is closed, as in
      Section 5.5.

7.  OCP over QUIC

7.1.  Establishing a Session

   The client completes the QUIC handshake, offering "ocp/1" through
   ALPN, validates the server certificate as in Section 4.4, opens the
   first client-initiated bidirectional stream and writes HELLO as the
   first octets of it.  The server writes WELCOME on the same stream.
   The rules of Section 5.1 apply, with the stream of Section 7.2 in
   place of the connection.

7.2.  The Control Stream

   One bidirectional stream carries the session.  It is the first
   client-initiated bidirectional stream of the connection, and every
   frame that has no other home travels on it: HELLO, WELCOME, CALL,
   RESULT, EVENT, SUBSCRIBE, UNSUBSCRIBE, BYE, ERROR, ACK and the state
   frames of a tunnel that has no stream of its own.

   *  There is exactly one OCP session per QUIC connection and exactly
      one control stream per session.

   *  A frame that arrives on another stream before WELCOME has been
      answered is a protocol error: the receiver MUST close the
      connection with the application error code ocp-stream-error
      (Appendix A.1).

   *  The control stream is not closed while the session lives.  An
      endpoint that closes it ends the session; the correct way to end a
      session is Section 7.10.

   *  Frames on the control stream are framed as in Section 4.6: back to
      back, delimited by length, with no relation to the boundaries of
      QUIC STREAM frames.

7.3.  Tunnels on Their Own Streams

   A tunnel MAY be given a stream of its own, which is what a tunnel
   wants: QUIC then does the flow control, the loss recovery and the
   ordering, and a stalled firmware transfer cannot delay a call.  The
   mapping is OPTIONAL and both endpoints MUST agree to it; when they do
   not, tunnel frames travel on the control stream as they do over TLS.
   Section 7.6 makes the mapping REQUIRED for a session that hands
   tunnel flow control to QUIC.

   1.  The opener sends TUNSTAT with "open" on the control stream and
       the peer answers, exactly as in [RFC0004], Section 6.6.2.

   2.  The opener opens one bidirectional stream.  The first frame on it
       MUST be TUNDATA or TUNSTAT of the tunnel the stream belongs to,
       and it is that frame's "tun" member that tells the peer which
       tunnel the stream carries.

   3.  Every frame on the stream thereafter MUST name the same tunnel.
       A frame that names another is rejected with E_UNSUPPORTED.

   4.  The end of a stream is the end of that direction of the tunnel: a
       sender that has no more to send sets "eof" and finishes the
       stream.  A receiver that has read it to the end has observed the
       end of that direction.

   5.  A tunnel is destroyed by TUNSTAT with "close" on the control
       stream, as in [RFC0004].  Either endpoint MAY instead reset the
       stream, which resets the tunnel: the peer reports it as
       E_TUNNEL_RESET, and the tunnel's name becomes reusable once both
       directions are finished.

   The messages and the members are unchanged.  A tunnel on its own
   stream still carries "tun", "off", "ack" and "window", so an
   implementation that ignores streams entirely still works and an
   implementation that uses them needs no new message.

7.4.  Datagrams

   Where both endpoints have negotiated the QUIC datagram extension
   [IETF-RFC9221], a datagram MAY carry one OCP frame for traffic that
   tolerates loss.

   *  One datagram carries exactly one frame, and the frame is complete:
      the length is in the header, so no reassembly is needed.

   *  The rules of [RFC0004], Section 7.2, apply: F_ACK requests an
      acknowledgement, a frame SHOULD NOT exceed 1200 octets including
      header and QUIC overhead, and bulk data belongs on a stream.

   *  HELLO and WELCOME MUST NOT be carried by a datagram: the session
      is established on the control stream.

   *  A datagram is not retransmitted by QUIC.  A frame that must arrive
      uses a stream; a frame that may be lost, such as a telemetry
      event, uses a datagram.

   *  An endpoint that has not negotiated the extension, or that
      receives a datagram before the handshake is complete, ignores the
      datagram.  It MUST NOT treat it as a protocol error.

7.5.  Flow Control

   QUIC flow control applies per stream and per connection, and OCP flow
   control applies per tunnel.  By default both apply.

   *  A sender MUST respect both: it MUST NOT transmit beyond the QUIC
      credit of the stream, and MUST NOT transmit beyond "ack + window"
      of the tunnel.

   *  When a tunnel has a stream of its own, an endpoint MAY advertise a
      tunnel window large enough never to bind, and rely on QUIC.  It
      MUST still send "ack", so that the peer can release the data it
      has sent.

   *  A receiver that stops reading a stream stops the sender, which is
      the same effect as a window of zero and is not an error.

   Section 7.6 turns the second of these off by agreement, for a
   deployment that would rather have one flow controller than two.

7.6.  Taking Over Tunnel Flow Control

   Two flow controllers over one tunnel is one more than a tunnel needs:
   the sender stops at whichever binds first, and tuning one without the
   other does nothing.  A session MAY therefore agree that QUIC flow
   control replaces the built-in window of [RFC0004], Section 6.6, for
   every tunnel of that session.  The mode is negotiated, once, in the
   same exchange as every other session property.

   The initiator offers it in the "quic" member of HELLO, as a list of
   the modes it implements, most preferred first; the responder states
   the agreed mode in the same member of WELCOME.

      "quic": {"tun_flow": ["quic"]}

      "quic": {"tun_flow": "quic"}

   The member and its key are OPTIONAL.  An absent member, a key that
   the responder does not confirm, or an empty list means the default,
   "ocp": the built-in window of [RFC0004] applies and Section 7.5
   applies as written.  A name an endpoint does not recognise MUST be
   ignored in an offer and MUST NOT be confirmed.

   +==============+==============+=====================================+
   |Member        |Type          |Meaning                              |
   +==============+==============+=====================================+
   |quic.tun_flow |array of text |HELLO only.  Modes offered: "ocp" or |
   |              |              |"quic".                              |
   +--------------+--------------+-------------------------------------+
   |quic.tun_flow |text          |WELCOME only.  The agreed mode.      |
   +--------------+--------------+-------------------------------------+

               Table 2: Member Added to HELLO and WELCOME

   When "quic" is the agreed mode:

   1.  Every tunnel of the session MUST be given a stream of its own
       (Section 7.3).  A peer that cannot MUST NOT confirm the mode.
       This is what makes the substitution work: QUIC controls a stream,
       and a tunnel that shares the control stream with everything else
       has no stream of its own to be controlled.

   2.  The built-in window no longer limits transmission.  A sender MUST
       NOT stop transmitting because of "window", and a receiver MUST
       NOT rely on "window" to make it stop.  Both MUST still carry the
       member, because the message of [RFC0004] is unchanged, and the
       RECOMMENDED value to carry is the largest one.

   3.  "ack" is still sent and still marks what has been received, so
       that a transfer can resume after a restart (Section 3).  It is no
       longer what paces the sender.

   4.  Back-pressure comes from QUIC: from the credit of the tunnel's
       stream, from the credit of the connection, and from the receiver
       simply not reading, which Section 7.5 already describes.

   5.  A tunnel is reset, closed and destroyed exactly as in Section 7.3
       and in [RFC0004], Section 6.6.  Resetting the stream resets the
       tunnel and is reported as E_TUNNEL_RESET.

   A sender that respects only one of the two controllers is broken in
   one direction or the other, so the mode is a property of the session
   and not of a tunnel: an endpoint MUST NOT apply it to some tunnels
   and not to others, and MUST NOT change it while the session lives.

   Over TLS and over DTLS the "quic" member is meaningless and MUST NOT
   be sent: there is one stream, or none, and nothing per tunnel to hand
   the work to.

7.7.  Early Data

   QUIC early data (0-RTT) MAY be used, subject to what it costs.

   *  A client MUST NOT send HELLO in early data unless the server has
      accepted it before, and MUST NOT send a CALL in early data whose
      effect is not idempotent.  A call that moves a machine is not
      idempotent.

   *  A server that accepts early data MUST apply the replay protection
      of [IETF-RFC9001].  A server SHOULD reject early data altogether
      when the session authenticates the initiator or when it serves
      actuators, which is to say in most deployments of this protocol.

   *  Early data carries no guarantee of uniqueness: an implementation
      MUST NOT assume that a frame that arrived in early data arrived
      once.

7.8.  Connection Migration

   QUIC may move a connection to a new address without the session
   noticing, which is a property OCP inherits.

   *  A migration does not end the session and does not change the
      identity of the peer, which is bound to the certificate and not to
      the address.

   *  An endpoint that authorises by address MUST re-check after a
      migration, because the address it authorised is gone.

   *  A server MAY disable migration for clients it does not expect to
      move, which is the common case for a device on a fixed network.

   *  A server that requires the initiator to authenticate again after a
      migration sends a new WELCOME only by establishing a new session,
      since a session has one WELCOME.  It SHOULD instead close the
      connection.

7.9.  Idle Timeout and Heartbeat

   The QUIC idle timeout MUST be longer than the interval of the OCP
   heartbeat of [RFC0004], Section 6.7, or a quiet session is closed by
   a transport that thinks it is idle.

   *  The RECOMMENDED heartbeat interval is five seconds and the
      RECOMMENDED idle timeout thirty seconds; a deployment that wants
      quieter traffic raises both together.

   *  An endpoint MAY send QUIC PING frames to keep a connection open
      when it has nothing to say.  A heartbeat is still RECOMMENDED,
      because it measures the peer and not only the path.

   *  A session that reaches the idle timeout ends without BYE, as in
      Section 5.5.

7.10. Closure

   A graceful close ends the session and then the connection.

   1.  The endpoint sends BYE on the control stream.

   2.  It closes the connection with CONNECTION_CLOSE carrying the
       application error code ocp-no-error (Appendix A.1).

   Either step alone ends the session: a BYE followed by a reset, or a
   CONNECTION_CLOSE with no BYE, both end it.  A transport error, as
   opposed to an application error, ends it too and reports a transport
   failure rather than an OCP one.

8.  Error Mapping

9.  Security Considerations

   *  Validation of the server certificate is mandatory (Section 4.4).
      An implementation MUST NOT offer an option to skip it, and MUST
      NOT fall back to cleartext when validation fails: a robot is a
      machine that can be told to move, and an unauthenticated session
      to it is worse than no session.

   *  TLS 1.2 is permitted (Section 5.3) because a device that cannot be
      updated should not be unreachable, and it is permitted only with
      an AEAD suite, with the extended master secret and without
      renegotiation.  A deployment SHOULD record which sessions are on
      1.2, and SHOULD plan to leave it.  Every version before 1.2 MUST
      NOT be used.

   *  A datagram transport can be made to answer for an address it has
      never seen.  DTLS therefore requires the cookie exchange
      (Section 6.1), and a deployment SHOULD NOT expose a device that
      speaks only DTLS to an untrusted network.

   *  Early data replays (Section 7.7).  The default is to refuse it.  A
      deployment that enables it has to know which of its calls are safe
      to repeat, which for a machine is very few.

   *  Migration moves a session to a new address (Section 7.8).  An
      endpoint that authorises by address is then authorising an address
      it never saw, and MUST re-check.

   *  Handing tunnel flow control to QUIC (Section 7.6) removes a limit
      and not a guarantee: the peer is still bounded by the credit of
      the stream and of the connection, and an endpoint that reads
      nothing still stops the sender.  An implementation MUST NOT treat
      the mode as permission to buffer without bound.

   *  A relay sees cleartext (Section 5.6).  The channel binding of
      Section 4.5 does not change that; it only stops a credential from
      being forwarded through one.

   *  The transport protects the octets between two endpoints, and
      nothing after them.  A frame that is logged, stored or forwarded
      by the application is cleartext again, and carries no proof of its
      own.  A deployment that needs a frame to be verifiable later wants
      [RFC0009] instead.

   *  ALPN is not access control.  Selecting "ocp/1" says what protocol
      follows and not who may use it; authorisation is Section 4.4 and
      the application.

   *  An endpoint SHOULD bind the loopback address (IPv4 127.0.0.1 or
      IPv6 ::1) unless remote access is intended, and a device SHOULD
      accept a secure transport with a pinned certificate rather than a
      shared token.
